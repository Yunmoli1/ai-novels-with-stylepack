# Arthur Conan Doyle — exemplars (en)

> Fifteen short extracts from the calibration samples, one per imitable move. Every
> quotation is verbatim except for stripped italic underscores; keep them out of
> generated text as prose to imitate.

### 1. The frame that names the subject

> To Sherlock Holmes she is always the woman. I have seldom heard him mention her under any other name.

Note: opening paragraph of a case. Two declarative sentences: the first fixes the man in
the reader's mind, the second proves he is worth fixing. Retrospective tense, no
adjectives. Use it to open a story whose centre is a person, not a crime.

### 2. The body measured at the door

> A man entered who could hardly have been less than six feet six inches in height, with the chest and limbs of a Hercules.

Note: entry by measurement. Height, then build, then a classical comparison — three
facts and one allusion in a single sentence. Never "an imposing man"; always the inches.

### 3. A conclusion that arrives before its reason

> “Wedlock suits you,” he remarked. “I think, Watson, that you have put on seven and a half pounds since I saw you.”

Note: the detective speaks first, the speaker tag sits between the two halves, and the
proof is a number with a fraction in it. The fraction is the joke — precision as humour.

### 4. The deduction chain, in public

> “I see it, I deduce it. How do I know that you have been getting yourself very wet lately, and that you have a most clumsy and careless servant girl?”

Note: the chain is delivered as questions, one fact per clause, no hedging and no
explanation of the reasoning. The reader is invited to catch up or not. Note the tagless
dialogue: the detective needs no `he said`.

### 5. The maxim, stated once

> “You see, but you do not observe. The distinction is clear. For example, you have frequently seen the steps which lead up from the hall to this room.”

Note: a lesson in three sentences — axiom, restatement, concrete instance. This is the
pack's most imitable structure for any scene where an expert teaches a novice.

### 6. The method as aphorism, then the snap back

> “I have no data yet. It is a capital mistake to theorise before one has data. Insensibly one begins to twist facts to suit theories, instead of theories to suit facts. But the note itself.

Note: an excerpt — the speech continues after `itself`. The shape is what matters: a
refusal, a maxim with `capital` in it, then an abrupt return to the object in the hand.
Doyle never lets a truth stand alone for more than a sentence.

### 7. Fog as staging, not mood

> A thick fog rolled down between the lines of dun-coloured houses, and the opposing windows loomed like dark, shapeless blurs through the heavy yellow wreaths.

Note: the weather is doing work. `Dun-coloured`, `shapeless blurs`, `heavy yellow
wreaths` — three visual facts that all say "you cannot see far". One simile, at the end,
never at the start.

### 8. The hour fixed by lamps

> It was already dusk, and the lamps were just being lighted as we paced up and down in front of Briony Lodge, waiting for the coming of its occupant.

Note: time, place and waiting in one sentence, with a proper name to anchor it. The
`already` and `just being` do the tense work; no clock is mentioned anywhere.

### 9. Clipped orders before action

> But I hear the rumble of wheels. It is her carriage. Now carry out my orders to the letter.”

Note: five-to-seven-word sentences, present tense inside dialogue, and a command as the
last beat. Adjectives: none. This is the rhythm to use whenever the plot starts moving.

### 10. The joke that releases tension

> “Well, moonshine is a brighter thing than fog,” said Holmes, laughing.

Note: seventy words would have ruined it. A pun on the client's metaphor, a laugh, and
the scene continues. Holmes's humour is dry, self-contained and usually at someone
else's expense.

### 11. The narrator's honest smallness

> I trust that I am not more dense than my neighbours, but I was always oppressed with a sense of my own stupidity in my dealings with Sherlock Holmes.

Note: self-deprecation with dignity — the comparison to neighbours protects the
narrator, `oppressed` admits the cost. Use once per story, usually early, to license the
reader's own confusion.

### 12. The deflating close

> “I confess that I have been as blind as a mole, but it is better to learn wisdom late than never to learn it at all.”

Note: the detective admits error in a proverb-shaped sentence. Emotion is named only as
`confess`; the rest is a maxim. This is how a case ends when the detective was outwitted
for a while.

### 13. The last line that opens a smaller door

> “I’ve heard that voice before,” said Holmes, staring down the dimly lit street. “Now, I wonder who the deuce that could have been.”

Note: the case is closed, and the final sentence is a new question with a mild oath in
it. `The deuce` dates the voice and keeps it light. Endings here are dry, and they
rarely explain.

### 14. Formal courtesy, which replaces description

> “Pray take a seat,” said Holmes. “This is my friend and colleague, Dr. Watson, who is occasionally good enough to help me in my cases. Whom have I the honour to address?”

Note: three sentences of pure manners, and in them: the invitation, the introduction, the
credentials of the narrator, the question that starts the case. `Pray`, `honour`, the
abbreviated `Dr.` — the period is in the grammar, not in the adjectives. Politeness here
is a form of characterisation.

### 15. The maxim with a laugh attached

> “There is nothing more deceptive than an obvious fact,” he answered, laughing.

Note: the tag does double work — `answered` keeps the exchange moving, `laughing`
pre-empts the reader's objection. Doyle's aphorisms are often laughed off in the same
breath, which keeps them from sounding like sermons. One aphorism per case.
